## The short version

Most account theft is not magic. Attackers usually trick people, reuse leaked passwords, or push fake alerts until someone gives up a code.

The fix is also not magic: unique passwords, multi-factor authentication, and a habit of going directly to apps instead of trusting links.

## How the attack usually works

An attacker starts with your email address or phone number. They try passwords from old breaches. If that fails, they send a convincing message that makes you believe something is urgent.

The message may say your package failed, your bank is locked, your cloud storage is full, or your account will be deleted.

## What this means for you

If you reuse passwords, one old breach can become many new break-ins. If you share login codes, even a strong password can be bypassed.

## What to do right now

- Put your email account behind the strongest protection first.
- Change reused passwords on banking, email, shopping, and social accounts.
- Turn on multi-factor authentication.
- Prefer app-based codes or security keys over text messages.
- Never read a login code to someone who called you.

## How to stay safe

Keep your routine boring. Use bookmarks or official apps. Let your password manager fill passwords only on real websites. If it does not offer to fill, stop and inspect the site.

## Red flags

- A message creates immediate panic.
- A link asks you to sign in again.
- Someone asks for a one-time code.
- A support agent asks you to install remote access software.

## Bottom line

Attackers win when they make you rush. Your best defense is a slower, cleaner login routine backed by unique passwords and stronger second factors.
